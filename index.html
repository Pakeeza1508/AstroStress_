The Problem

NASA has the sensors. What's missing is the brain.

Astronauts wear health monitors and the ISS tracks cabin air. But nothing connects these to predict stress or suggest actions.

What NASA already has

  • Heart rate & skin sensors on astronauts
  • CO₂, temperature, humidity data from ISS
  • Safety thresholds (NASA-STD-3001)

What's missing

  • AI that predicts stress from sensor data
  • Combining body signals with cabin conditions
  • Actionable alerts — not just numbers

    DATA SOURCE 01 · WESAD

    WESAD — Wearable Stress and Affect Detection

    Earth-based laboratory recordings from 15 participants, collected with chest ECG and wrist electrodermal activity (EDA) sensors. We extract 19 physiological features from valid 120-second windows for the four-model stress classification and leave-one-subject-out evaluation. These recordings are not astronaut data.

    DATA SOURCE 02 · CONFIDENCE RESEARCH

    Prediction Confidence & Reliability

    We extended our original WESAD stress-detection research to test how confidently the AI can identify stress and when it should be less certain. On the full test data, the model classified 88.65% correctly. When we considered only predictions with at least 80% confidence, accuracy reached 94.24% on approximately 80% of the test data. This helps demonstrate the trade-off between making more predictions and making more reliable predictions.

    DATA SOURCE 03 · NASA TELEMETRY

    NASA Environmental Telemetry Audit

    We used real NASA environmental sensor records from the RR-1 and RR-6 space research missions to study how CO₂, temperature, and humidity changed over time. We also checked for missing measurements and readings that stayed exactly the same for unusually long periods. Our result is an interactive environmental data-quality analysis that highlights patterns and possible gaps researchers would need to investigate before using such readings in a future space-health monitoring system.

    How should the AI explanation be interpreted?

    Understand the difference between a feature influencing a prediction and causing stress.

    SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) assigns each feature a value representing its contribution to the model's prediction for a specific window. Positive SHAP values push the prediction toward stress; negative values push toward no-stress.

    Why is explainability important?
    In safety-critical applications like astronaut health monitoring, it is not sufficient for a model to be accurate. Clinicians and mission planners need to understand why a stress classification was made, verify it against physiological intuition, and identify potential failure modes.
    Computation details
    SHAP values are computed using TreeExplainer on the best model from the corrected Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) evaluation. The local server can provide per-window in-sample SHAP for the saved final model. Attribution is not an average of Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) folds.

    NASA Environmental Data

    Real NASA Environmental Data Application exports from rodent-research hardware. This is an exploratory, descriptive data-quality audit — not astronaut stress data, a crew-cabin exposure assessment, or NASA-STD-3001 compliance testing.

    —Recorded observations

    Unfiltered export, including possible prelaunch/post-return records.

    —Rows with ISS-channel CO₂ > 4,000 ppm

    Exploratory row count, not a compliance violation or exposure duration.

    —Gaps longer than 10 minutes

    Missing time means sample counts cannot describe continuous exposure.

    Weekly median environmental signals

    Seven-day bins from each export's first timestamp; sampled medians, not duration-weighted. Select a parameter below.

    Measurement quality and limitations

    Extended identical-value runs and simultaneous flat sensor channels are flags for investigation, not evidence of defective hardware. No mission-phase filtering has been validated. RR-1 and RR-6 cover different years and cannot support causal comparisons.

    RR-9: the supplied file contains only 10 summary rows, not a timestamped timeline. Its radiation unit label is ambiguous; no dose-rate interpretation is made.

    Source: NASA EDA RR-1 / RR-6 telemetry CSVs and RR-9 summary CSV, SHA-256 checked against the source-data research audit. All numbers are descriptive; 4,000 ppm is a sensitivity cutoff, not a validated crew exposure threshold. Q-MEEF is a project working name.
